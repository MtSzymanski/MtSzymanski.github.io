Post 1 - 16/08/2026

I’m in Dresden as part of the 6-week Dive Into Research programme organised by Ben Hollering, Aida Maraj and Irem Portakal [also Kathleen Kulb on the administrative side!]. As part of this six weeks I will try to write a weekly blog post at the end of each week summarising what I got up to.

As I’m writing this, I have just finished my first week of the programme and I’ve had the privilege to meet and work with some incredible people. The first week has mainly focused on lectures and workshop sessions designed to bring everyone to a similar level before projects are assigned and the main research task begins. The material covered was on algebraic geometry and its computational applications to problems in statistics and game theory.

My project will be on studying Level-2 CFN Phylogenetic Networks and trying to answer questions surrounding the parameterization and identifiability of these models; I will be working together with Jakub Mazur, Francesco Nowell, as well as Ben Hollering on this. In many ways this serves as a continuation of my adventure studying the geometry of phylogenetic models which began with my work on them together with Dimitra Kosta (cf. Phylogenetic Algebraic Geometry and Matroid Applications in Phylogenetics Algebraic Geometry).

During the weekend I’ve went to the Dresden Canaletto festival (lot’s of live music and surprisingly Hungarian lángos) as well as on a hike with other DIR participants to Saxon-Switzerland. The latter was a good introduction to the German pastime of hiking and I’ll try later to upload some photos of the mesmerising views of the Malerweg.

Tomorrow, arguably, the real work of the research project begins. There’s a lot of reading ahead that needs to be done, and still lots more to learn about the syntax and functionalities of Macaulay2 and Oscar, but having said that I’m looking forward to the week ahead. Theirs is nothing more to say other than: “let’s calculate!”

Post 2 & 3 - 30/08/2026

So, week two and I’m already behind… anyhow, here’s a longer post covering both week two and three of my Dresden trip: happy reading!

Project wise, in week two, a lot of mine and Kuba’s time has been spent on trying to adapt/write our own Oscar code to compute examples for small level-2 CFN simple phylogenetic networks. For reference (I didn’t know this until I’ve started this project) Oscar is an open-source computer algebra system written in Julia which we’ll be using throughout this research project. There already exists a lot of Oscar code for analysing group-based models on level-1 phylogenetic networks, however these were incapable of analysing level-2 networks. After almost a week’s worth of work, and a lot of help from Ben, we managed to create our own functions to analyse level-2 networks: huzzah! I should also quickly explain what a simple phylogenetic network is and what a level-k phylogenetic network actually are, but instead of that I’ll employ the mathematician’s favourite trick and instead refer the curious reader to the following which (genuinely) provide good exposition to the theoretical material of this project. The references are [Elizabeth Gross, Colby Long, and Joseph Rusinko. Phylogenetic networks, 2019, link to arXiv ], and [Muhammad Ardiyansyah. Distinguishing level-2 phylogenetic networks using phylogenetic invariants, 2021, link to arXiv ]. Moreover, as part of the programme, every week we have to give a 20 minute presentation to the rest of the cohort summarising our progress for the week. Kuba and I gave an exposition to the study of phylogenetic models using algebraic geometry, and Kuba has immortalised by coining and using the phrase “waka-waka-MLE” to skip over the details of Maximum Likliehood Estimate calculation from a model parameterization. I have a feeling that similar antics will continue in future presentations and I’m looking forward to inserting some of my own suggestions!

During the weekend of week two, we (as in the research students) have organised a movie night and a Tischfußall tournament! Afterwards we went for Döner to Mamaris Kebap Haus which I was told won a best-döner-in-Germany competition, and spent the rest of the night chatting and walking around the Neustadt. On Sunday, I ventured to the North of the city: taking a walk in and enjoying the views of the Dredener Heide, seeing the Schillerhäuschen with a quick talk (in German of course) about Schiller, and walking along the Elbe.

Now that the Oscar code is finished, we were able to start computing examples and dive into the theory, or so we thought…

Monday morning, start of week three, we were met with a disaster, our code wasn’t working! We spent most of the day trying to diagnose the problem, it turned out Oscar was being particularly picky with how we defined the rings used for our model parameterisations, and spent the next day actually implementing the solution and finally computing some examples. Just in time for the project presentation, for which we had a special guest in the form of Bernd Sturmfels who came to watch. Not nerve-racking at all! The presentation went well enough and we got some good pointers from the audience on ideas to try for our project as well as questions we haven’t thought of yet. I also learned that Bernd is actually lovely to talk to, as most accomplished mathematicians tend to be, and we all took a group photo later outside the institute to mark the occasion.

The next day we revisited our calculations, corrected them where need be, and obtained some good results for 4 and 5 leaf simple level-2 networks; we have found that not all such networks on this small number of leaves are distinguishable (i.e. they have equal ideals, or more accurately the quadratic components of their ideals are equal because that’s what we’re checking), and the hope is that for 6 leaf networks all networks will be distinguishable but we are still to complete the calculations for this case. The curious reader may wonder why are we only checking the quadratic components of the ideals, the answer to which is two-fold: 1. It is computationally easier, but more importantly 2. Ben conjectures that the ideals of these models are quadratically generated, and so it suffices to only check the degree two components.

To finish off the week, Kuba and I have began to run the code for the 6 leaf simple networks and we began to read more about the theoretical results in the literature that have been recently established for level-2 networks to better understand what is available and how does our project fit into the existing body of work.

During the weekend, I had the pleasure of visiting the city of Leipzig and spending the whole day walking around the city, visiting the museums, and in general being amazed by the ornate architecture and grandeur of the city: it felt like strolling inside a Rococo artwork! Sunday on the other end was a much more relaxed day spent strolling around in the Großgarten and writing up a number of things, including this blog. With that let me say Gute Nacht, and I will update you on the 6-leaf results in the next post, Tschuss!

Post 4 - 06/09/2026

Week 4 has come and passed and I’m happy to report that Kuba and I have made some good progress!

First we have managed to generalise Theorem 2.11 in this paper to CFN models which meant that we could recover the main identifiability results from this paper on CFN models, and as a bonus these results hold for not just level-2 networks but for general level-k networks. Least to say that we’re quite chuffed, and next week we will foucs on generalising other results, or more frankly seeing why we can’t quite easily generalise them.

On the computational side of things, we’ve hit a bit of a wall; the code we have wrote struggles to compute the ideals of certain 6-leaf level-2 networks, which means we can’t see if all such network models are distinguishable for example. Ben is kindly looking over our code this week trying to optimise it (thank you Ben!) and hopefully next week we will be able to complete our 6-leaf network computations. On the other hand, closer examination of our results for 5-leaf networks revealed that some of the network ideals are not quadratically generated, with two examples having additional quartic generators. This is super weird! and to be frank none of us (this includes our supervisors) aren’t exactly sure why this happens, which makes this an exciting point to investigate next week.

Lastly, we are looking at using small networks which “sit-in” the bigger level-2 networks to obtain some identifiability results. For example we have found a family of level-2 networks which always contain the sunlet (level-1) networks and we’re trying to use the distinguishability results for sunlets to be able to distinguish the bigger network they sit inside of.

On the weekend I had the pleasure of going to the Semperoper, the opera house in Dresden for a production of the Magic Flute; in short it was phenomenal. The rest of the evening was spent in the bustling Neustadt where I was introduced to a surprisingly German invention: Mexikaner shots!

Hopefully week 5 will bring more new results and we will keep our momentum, bis bald!
